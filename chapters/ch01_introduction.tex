\chapter{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Relay communication uses intermediate nodes to extend coverage, improve reliability, and increase throughput in wireless systems. Classical strategies such as amplify-and-forward (\texttt{AF}) and decode-and-forward (\texttt{DF}) are well-established reference protocols. In canonical relay-channel models, block-based \texttt{DF} can achieve or approach capacity with asymptotically long codewords \cite{CoverGamal1979RelayChannel,ElGamalKim2011NetworkIT,Laneman2004CooperativeDiversity,RankovWittneben2007HalfDuplexRelay,Nosratinia2004CooperativeCommunication}. As clarified in Remark~\ref{rem:df-terminology}, these results concern coded block operation and do not directly characterize the uncoded, symbol-wise setting studied here.

Recent advances in machine learning have motivated neural approaches to physical-layer detection, decoding, and end-to-end optimization, particularly under nonlinear, low-SNR, mismatched, or otherwise non-ideal conditions. This motivates the central research question:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Under what conditions can neural network--based relays improve upon or complement classical relay strategies in two-hop relay communication?}
\end{quote}

This question is investigated within a unified simulation framework. Nine relay methods are implemented (Section~\ref{sec:relay-strategies}), eight of which are evaluated in the canonical comparison (Section~\ref{sec:siso-bpsk-performance-baseline-relay-comparison}): two classical strategies (\texttt{AF} and \texttt{DF}) and six learned relays---\texttt{MLP}, SNR-adaptive \texttt{Hybrid}, variational autoencoder (\texttt{VAE}), \texttt{Transformer}, \texttt{Mamba-S6}, and \texttt{Mamba-2 SSD}. A conditional GAN (\texttt{cGAN} with \texttt{WGAN-GP}) is implemented but excluded from the canonical comparison on computational-cost grounds.

The objective is not to propose a new relay architecture, but to determine where learning helps, where it does not, and why. As formalized in Chapter~\ref{sec:research-objectives}, the question has two dimensions. Without resource constraints, the relevant boundary is determined by detection accuracy and the \emph{reliability of the channel estimate}. Under latency and computational constraints, the boundary becomes \emph{arithmetic}. These boundaries need not coincide, and their separation is one of the thesis's results. Section~\ref{sec:joint-latency-memory} examines this interaction for channels with memory, where trellis complexity grows as $M^{L}$ and block-based relaying introduces buffering requirements.

\paragraph{Method: one assumption at a time.}
The investigation proceeds through four layers that progressively relax channel assumptions while using the strongest evaluated classical baseline appropriate to each setting.

At \textbf{layer~1} (Chapter~\ref{sec:experiments}), the channel is memoryless and perfectly known. This layer asks whether learning improves upon a classical rule that already implements the MAP symbol decision for the assumed channel. \textbf{Layer~2} (Chapter~\ref{sec:unknown-channels}) introduces channel memory while retaining channel knowledge, isolating the required relay function class and its computational cost; Section~\ref{sec:qpsk-unknown-channel} re-examines this setting with QPSK. \textbf{Layer~3} replaces perfect channel knowledge with an estimate obtained from a finite pilot budget at one measured SNR, making channel-estimate reliability an explicit experimental variable. \textbf{Layer~4} removes per-block CSI by redrawing a realization from the same impairment family for every block while retaining prior knowledge of that family through training, and compares the learned relay with blind classical methods.

Together, these layers address the central research question by progressively relaxing the \emph{preconditions} of the classical methods. Each baseline is evaluated under its stated assumptions; in particular, matched MLSE is optimal for sequence error rather than necessarily for BER. Hypotheses H1--H4 are evaluated at layer~1 and H5 across layers~2--4.

A methodological finding also emerges from this process (Section~\ref{sec:conclusions}): an apparent architecture-dependent sensitivity to random initialization is traced to a single silent optimization failure whose training loss differs by approximately an order of magnitude despite remaining undetected by the monitored validation accuracy.

\section{Scope and Contributions}\label{sec:system-model-intro}

The canonical system is the half-duplex two-hop SISO relay link summarized in Table~\ref{tbl:scope} and defined formally in Section~\ref{sec:system-model-1}: one relay, no direct source--destination path, i.i.d.\ Rayleigh fast fading on both hops, complex-baseband Gray-coded QPSK, and uncoded BER. In the canonical comparison, only the relay function $f_\theta(\cdot)$ is varied; the source, channel model, and destination processing are fixed.

The simulator is calibrated against the closed-form two-hop symbol-wise DF result on the canonical channel. After the QPSK $E_s/N_0$-to-$E_b/N_0$ conversion, simulated BER differs from the analytical result by less than $3.6\%$ at every tabulated SNR (Table~\ref{tbl:table2}).

Beyond the canonical comparison, the thesis contains two extensions. The supplementary coded study (Sections~\ref{sec:coded-block-df}--\ref{sec:coded-latency-capacity}) evaluates finite-length block-DF, soft-information forwarding, equal-$E_s/N_0$ and equal-throughput comparisons, adaptive coding, and a latency constraint. The principal extension, Chapter~\ref{sec:unknown-channels}, progressively relaxes the canonical channel assumptions and determines where a minimal learned relay complements the strongest classical baseline evaluated and where it does not (H5).

Figure~\ref{fig:canonical-relay-benchmark} summarizes the canonical benchmark. Its components are identical as \emph{models}, not as instantaneous realizations: because the fading is fast, $h_1[i]$ and $h_2[i]$ are independently redrawn for each symbol.
