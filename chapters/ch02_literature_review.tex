\chapter{Background and Related Work}\label{sec:literature-review}

\paragraph{Scope relative to the canonical model.}
Unless stated otherwise, this chapter reviews relay-processing methods in the context of the canonical memoryless channel model used in Chapter~\ref{sec:experiments}. Channel memory, sequence detection, and intersymbol interference (ISI) are treated separately in Chapter~\ref{sec:unknown-channels}.

\section{Classical Relay Strategies}\label{sec:classical-relay-strategies}

This thesis asks whether a learned relay function \(f_\theta(\cdot)\) can complement classical relay processing when channel assumptions are mismatched or the required mapping is difficult to specify analytically. On the matched memoryless channel, the relevant symbol statistics are already captured by the classical MAP symbol detector; any advantage of learning must therefore be established empirically rather than assumed.

\subsection{Amplify-and-Forward (AF)}\label{sec:classical-relay-strategies-af}

AF scales the received signal to satisfy a target relay power:
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:af-gain}
G = \sqrt{\frac{P_{\text{target}}}{\mathbb{E}[|y_R|^2]}} .
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad af-gain}

For CSI-assisted variable-gain AF, the standard dual-hop end-to-end SNR is \cite{Laneman2004CooperativeDiversity,TseViswanath2005Fundamentals}
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:af-snr}
\text{SNR}_{\text{eff}}^{\text{AF}}
=
\frac{\text{SNR}_1\text{SNR}_2}
{\text{SNR}_1+\text{SNR}_2+1}.
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad af-snr}

This expression assumes instantaneous CSI-assisted variable gain. The implementation in this thesis instead uses one block-adaptive scalar estimated from empirical received power. It is therefore neither conventional statistical fixed-gain AF nor per-symbol CSI-assisted variable-gain AF. Equations~\ref{eq:af-snr}--\ref{eq:af-p-eff} are included as standard background; all simulated AF results are obtained by Monte Carlo evaluation.

AF forwards both signal and first-hop noise, so end-to-end performance remains constrained by the weaker hop.

\subsection{Decode-and-Forward (DF)}\label{sec:classical-relay-strategies-df}

DF detects the received symbols and retransmits regenerated symbols:
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:df-demod-remod}
\hat{b}_R=\text{demod}(y_R),
\qquad
x_R=\text{mod}(\hat{b}_R).
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad df-demod-remod}

For independent hops, the end-to-end BER is
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:df-ber-hops}
P_e^{\text{DF}}
=
P_{e,1}(1-P_{e,2})
+
(1-P_{e,1})P_{e,2},
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad df-ber-hops}
or equivalently
\[
P_e^{\text{DF}}
=
P_{e,1}+P_{e,2}-2P_{e,1}P_{e,2}.
\]

For BPSK under the real-valued AWGN convention used here,
\(P_e=Q(\sqrt{\text{SNR}})\). DF avoids forwarding first-hop noise but propagates incorrect first-hop decisions. As clarified in Remark~\ref{rem:df-terminology}, the canonical DF baseline is an uncoded symbol-wise detector, not finite-length coded block-DF; its optimality claims are therefore restricted to that setting.

\subsection{Other Classical Relay Techniques}

AF and DF represent two canonical relay operations: AF forwards a scaled observation, whereas DF regenerates a detected symbol or decoded block. Compress-forward, estimate-forward, and compute-forward address different network models and objectives and are not intermediate points on a single AF--DF continuum. Accordingly, AF and symbol-wise DF are used here as representative canonical baselines, not as performance bounds or an exhaustive characterization of classical relaying.

\section{Machine Learning in Wireless Communication}\label{sec:machine-learning-in-wireless-communication}

Machine learning has been applied to physical-layer tasks including channel estimation \cite{YeLiJuang2018OFDMDL}, signal detection \cite{SamuelDiskinWunder2019MIMODetect}, end-to-end communication \cite{Dorner2018OverTheAirDL}, and resource allocation \cite{Sun2018LearnToOptimize}. Its relevance to relay processing lies in learning nonlinear mappings directly from data when analytical models are unavailable, mismatched, or computationally inconvenient.

\subsection{Theoretical Basis: Universal Approximation and Denoising}\label{sec:theoretical-basis-universal-approximation-and-denoising}

Universal-approximation results \cite{Cybenko1989UAT,Hornik1991UAT,GoodfellowBengioCourville2016DeepLearning} establish that feedforward networks with suitable nonlinear activations can approximate continuous functions on compact domains given sufficient capacity. These are existence results and do not guarantee finite-sample learning, successful optimization, or generalization.

For squared-error loss, the Bayes-optimal estimator is the conditional mean
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:optimal-denoiser}
f^*(y_{i-w},\ldots,y_{i+w})
=
\mathbb{E}[x_i\mid y_{i-w},\ldots,y_{i+w}] .
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad optimal-denoiser}

For BPSK over AWGN,
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:optimal-denoiser-awgn}
f^*(y)=\tanh\left(\frac{y}{\sigma^2}\right).
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad optimal-denoiser-awgn}

For fixed \(\sigma^2\), this posterior mean can be represented exactly by a suitably scaled tanh unit. The learned relay used here, however, is trained at SNR \(\in\{5,10,15\}\)~dB and evaluated over \(0\)--\(20\)~dB; under fading it must additionally accommodate a distribution of instantaneous effective SNRs. Its ability to approximate this family is therefore an empirical question rather than a consequence of the fixed-SNR expression. Moreover, posterior-mean optimality applies to local mean-squared error (MSE) and provides qualitative intuition rather than a theorem about end-to-end BER.

The bias--variance decomposition provides a complementary view of model complexity:
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:bias-variance-decomp}
\mathbb{E}[(\hat{x}-x)^2]
=
\text{Bias}^2(\hat{x})
+
\text{Var}(\hat{x})
+
\sigma^2_{\text{irreducible}} .
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad bias-variance-decomp}

Increasing model capacity can alter both bias and variance, but modern regularized networks need not follow a monotonic classical trade-off. Because the memoryless denoising target is low-dimensional and smooth, additional capacity may provide limited benefit. Section~\ref{sec:capacity-and-overfitting} evaluates this question empirically without inferring overfitting in the absence of train--test-gap evidence.

\subsection{Prior Work in Deep Learning for Physical-Layer Processing}\label{sec:prior-work-in-deep-learning-for-physical-layer-processing}

Ye et al.~\cite{YeLiJuang2018OFDMDL} applied DNNs to joint channel estimation and signal detection in OFDM using pilot and data observations. Dorner et al.~\cite{Dorner2018OverTheAirDL} formulated the communication chain as an autoencoder trained end-to-end with a differentiable reconstruction objective and evaluated by error rate.

In relay-assisted systems, much learning-based work focuses on relay selection or resource allocation while retaining conventional relay processing \cite{Akdemir2024DLRelaySelection}. More closely related approaches embed learning within model-based detectors. Tsai et al.~\cite{TsaiTeng2020BCJRNet} replace channel-dependent BCJR branch likelihoods with a neural model, and Karanov et al.~\cite{Karanov2024BurstyISI} extend this approach to ISI channels with bursty impulsive noise. Chen et al.~\cite{Chen2024BCJRNetRobustness} report advantages under imperfect channel knowledge, closely related to the channel-estimate reliability regime examined in Chapter~\ref{sec:unknown-channels}.

These studies use BCJR rather than the maximum-likelihood sequence estimation (MLSE) baseline emphasized in this thesis; the two optimize different decision criteria. BCJR is included as an additional QPSK benchmark in Section~\ref{sec:bcjr-benchmark}; the corresponding BPSK comparison is outside the evaluated scope.

Broader reviews of machine learning in wireless systems are provided in \cite{Gunduz2019MLAir}. A distinct line of work interprets AF relay networks as neural structures and optimizes analog relay gains to exploit hardware nonlinearities \cite{Bergel2023RelaysAsNeurons,BergelICASSP2024}; this differs from the symbol-level relay processing studied here.

\subsection{Neural Network Relay Processing}\label{sec:neural-network-relay-processing}

The supervised learned relay estimates \(f_\theta\) by minimizing empirical MSE:
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:mse-loss}
\mathcal{L}(\theta)
=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\hat{x}_i-x_i)^2,
\qquad
\hat{x}_i=f_\theta(y_{i-w:i+w}).
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad mse-loss}

A representative MLP relay is
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:mlp-relay}
\mathbf{h}
=
\text{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}_1\mathbf{w}+\mathbf{b}_1),
\qquad
\hat{x}
=
\tanh(\mathbf{W}_2\mathbf{h}+\mathbf{b}_2).
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad mlp-relay}

Under the canonical i.i.d.\ fast-fading, white-noise model, adjacent symbols are statistically independent and \(y_R[i]\) is sufficient for estimating \(x[i]\); a window therefore provides no theoretical advantage. The fixed window is retained because Chapter~\ref{sec:unknown-channels} introduces a three-tap ISI channel for which neighboring observations are informative. Holding the architecture fixed helps isolate the effect of changing channel assumptions.

Training at multiple SNRs (5, 10, and 15~dB) produces one estimator for a distribution of operating conditions. The objective minimizes average risk under the sampled SNR distribution, not worst-case risk over the interval.

Model complexity is therefore an empirical question: additional parameters may improve approximation but may also be unnecessary for a low-dimensional denoising function. This trade-off is evaluated in Section~\ref{sec:capacity-and-overfitting}.

\section{Generative Models for Signal Processing}\label{sec:generative-models-for-signal-processing}

Generative models provide an alternative inductive bias by modeling latent structure rather than only a direct input--output mapping. In probabilistic form,
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:bayes-rule}
p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y})
\propto
p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})p(\mathbf{x}).
\end{equation}

Their relevance here is empirical: whether this additional structure improves relay denoising relative to direct supervised estimation.

\subsection{Variational Autoencoders}\label{sec:variational-autoencoders}

A variational autoencoder (VAE) \cite{KingmaWelling2014VAE} introduces a latent variable
\(\mathbf{z}\sim p(\mathbf{z})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})\), a decoder \(p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})\), and an approximate posterior \(q_\phi(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})\). The log evidence decomposes as
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:vae-elbo}
\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x})
=
\underbrace{
\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}
\left[
\log
\frac{p_\theta(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})}
{q_\phi(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})}
\right]}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}
+
\underbrace{
D_{KL}
\left(
q_\phi(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})
\|
p_\theta(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})
\right)}_{\geq 0}.
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad vae-elbo}

A \(\beta\)-VAE can be trained by minimizing the negative ELBO:
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:vae-loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{VAE}}
=
-
\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})}
[\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})]
+
\beta
D_{KL}
\left(
q_\phi(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})
\|
p(\mathbf{z})
\right),
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad vae-loss}
where \(\beta\) controls the reconstruction--regularization trade-off \cite{Higgins2017betaVAE}.

The reparameterization trick expresses
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:reparameterization}
\mathbf{z}
\sim
\mathcal{N}
(\boldsymbol{\mu},\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2))
\end{equation}
as
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:reparameterization-sample}
\mathbf{z}
=
\boldsymbol{\mu}
+
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\odot\boldsymbol{\epsilon},
\qquad
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}),
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad reparameterization}
allowing gradients to propagate through the stochastic latent representation.

The relay implemented here is a supervised denoising VAE: its encoder receives noisy observations and reconstruction is evaluated against clean targets. Its denoising behavior therefore follows from the noisy-input/clean-target objective rather than from the standard unconditional VAE formulation alone. The relay samples \(\mathbf{z}\) at inference; a deterministic posterior-mean variant is not evaluated.

\subsection{Generative vs.~Discriminative Paradigms for Relay Processing}\label{sec:generative-vs.-discriminative-paradigms-for-relay-processing}

For the signal alphabets considered here, whether a generative latent representation improves relay denoising over direct supervised regression is not evident a priori. This thesis therefore evaluates the VAE, supervised learned relays, and classical baselines under a common experimental framework. The corresponding results are reported in Chapter~\ref{sec:experiments}.

A conditional GAN relay is implemented and described in Chapter~\ref{sec:methods} but excluded from the canonical comparison on computational-cost grounds; no performance claim is made for it.

\section{Sequence Models: Transformers and State Space Models}\label{sec:sequence-models-transformers-and-state-space-models}

Transformers and structured state space models (SSMs) provide alternative mechanisms for sequence processing. Their relevance to this thesis is primarily architectural: the canonical channel is memoryless, whereas the unknown-channel experiments introduce temporal dependence through ISI.

\subsection{Transformers and the Attention Mechanism}\label{sec:transformers-and-the-attention-mechanism}

Transformers \cite{Vaswani2017Attention} use self-attention to model interactions among sequence positions:
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:attention}
\text{Attention}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V})
=
\text{softmax}
\left(
\frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{T}}{\sqrt{d_k}}
\right)
\mathbf{V}.
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad attention}

Multi-head attention applies independent projections in parallel:
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:multihead-attention}
\text{MultiHead}(\mathbf{X})
=
\text{Concat}(\text{head}_1,\ldots,\text{head}_h)W^O,
\qquad
\text{head}_i
=
\text{Attention}
(\mathbf{X}W_i^Q,\mathbf{X}W_i^K,\mathbf{X}W_i^V).
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad multihead-attention}

The four projection matrices contribute
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:multihead-attention-parameters-count}
4d_{\text{model}}^2
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad multihead-attention-parameters-count}
parameters, excluding biases and the feedforward sublayer.

The attention matrix
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:multihead-attention-matrix}
\mathbf{A}
=
\text{softmax}
\left(
\frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{T}}{\sqrt{d_k}}
\right)
\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad multihead-attention-matrix}
requires \(O(n^2)\) pairwise interactions. For the \(n=11\) relay window used here, this cost is negligible.

Because self-attention alone does not encode absolute position, sinusoidal positional encoding is used:
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:positional-encoding}
PE_{(pos,2k)}
=
\sin\left(\frac{pos}{10000^{2k/d}}\right),
\qquad
PE_{(pos,2k+1)}
=
\cos\left(\frac{pos}{10000^{2k/d}}\right).
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad positional-encoding}

\subsection{Structured State Space Models}\label{sec:structured-state-space-models}

Structured SSMs \cite{GuGoelRe2022SSM} represent sequences through a discretized dynamical system. Starting from
\[
\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)
=
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}(t)+\mathbf{B}u(t),
\qquad
y(t)=\mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}(t)+Du(t),
\]
zero-order-hold discretization yields
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:ssm-discrete}
\mathbf{x}_k
=
\bar{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{x}_{k-1}
+
\bar{\mathbf{B}}u_k,
\qquad
y_k
=
\mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}_k+Du_k.
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad ssm-discrete}

Structured parameterizations such as S4 make this recurrence computationally efficient while retaining long-range state information \cite{GuGoelRe2022SSM}. Unlike full attention, SSM computation scales linearly with sequence length.

\subsection{Mamba and Mamba-2}\label{sec:mamba-selective-state-spaces}

Mamba \cite{gu2023mamba} makes selected SSM parameters input-dependent:
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:mamba-selective}
\Delta_k
=
\text{softplus}(W_\Delta u_k+b_\Delta),
\qquad
\mathbf{B}_k=W_Bu_k,
\qquad
\mathbf{C}_k=W_Cu_k.
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad mamba-selective}

This selectivity allows the state update to depend on the current input. Such temporal adaptation is relevant primarily when the channel introduces memory; the canonical channel provides no channel-induced temporal dependence for the state to exploit.

\begin{remark}
The adaptive-filtering interpretation becomes relevant in Chapter~\ref{sec:unknown-channels}. Under the canonical memoryless model, there is no channel-induced temporal structure for the state to track.
\end{remark}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{results/mamba_ssd_s6.png}
    \caption[Mamba: selective state spaces]{Structured SSMs propagate observations through a latent state; selective variants make the state transition input-dependent.}
    \label{fig:mamba_ssd_s6}
\end{figure}

Mamba-2 \cite{DaoGu2024TransformersSSM} reformulates the recurrence through structured state-space duality, enabling chunked matrix operations with state transfer between chunks. This reduces sequential computation for long sequences. Both Mamba variants are evaluated here, but with the short relay windows used in this thesis their distinction is architectural rather than a demonstrated computational advantage.

\subsection{State Space Models for Signal Processing}\label{sec:state-space-models-for-signal-processing}

SSMs provide a neural analogue of classical state-space filtering: fixed-parameter SSMs resemble learned linear filters, whereas selective models such as Mamba implement input-dependent state updates. This connection is most relevant when the channel introduces temporal structure.

No controlled comparison of Mamba-S6, Mamba-2 SSD, and Transformers for the same relay-processing task was identified in the literature reviewed for this thesis. Because the relay window is short (\(n=11\)), the long-sequence computational advantages of SSMs over quadratic attention \cite{DaoGu2024TransformersSSM} are not exercised here.

\section{Theoretical Foundations: Channel Models}\label{sec:theoretical-foundations-channel-models}

This section summarizes the analytical BER expressions used to validate the simulation framework and establish classical reference performance. These checks ensure that subsequent learned-relay comparisons use verified classical implementations.

\subsection{AWGN Channel: Theoretical Analysis}\label{sec:awgn-channel-theoretical-analysis}

The AWGN channel is
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:awgn-channel}
y=x+n,
\qquad
n\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2),
\qquad
\sigma^2=\frac{P_s}{\text{SNR}_{\text{linear}}},
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad awgn-channel}
with \(P_s=1\) for unit-power BPSK.

\subsubsection{Single-hop BER}\label{sec:awgn-single-hop-ber}

For BPSK under the real-noise convention used here,
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:awgn-ber}
P_e^{\text{AWGN}}
=
Q\left(\sqrt{\text{SNR}}\right)
=
\frac{1}{2}
\operatorname{erfc}
\left(
\sqrt{\frac{\text{SNR}}{2}}
\right).
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad awgn-ber}

\begin{remark}[Noise convention]\label{noise-convension}
The textbook expression \(Q(\sqrt{2E_b/N_0})\) assumes variance \(N_0/2\) per real dimension. Here, real AWGN has variance \(\sigma^2=P_s/\text{SNR}\), giving \(E_b/N_0=\text{SNR}/2\) and therefore \(Q(\sqrt{\text{SNR}})\).

The fading simulations instead use complex Gaussian noise with total power \(1/\text{SNR}\), or variance \(1/(2\,\text{SNR})\) per real dimension. After equalization, this yields the standard factor of \(2\) in the fading-channel Q-function argument.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{Two-hop AF BER}\label{sec:awgn-two-hop-af-ber}

For equal-SNR hops under the standard CSI-assisted AF model,
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:af-snr-eff}
\text{SNR}_{\text{eff}}^{\text{AF}}
=
\frac{\gamma^2}{2\gamma+1},
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad af-snr-eff}
where \(\gamma\) is the per-hop SNR. Under the real-noise convention,
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:af-p-eff}
P_e^{\text{AF}}
=
Q\left(\sqrt{\text{SNR}_{\text{eff}}}\right).
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad af-p-eff}

At high SNR,
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:gamma-high-snr}
\text{SNR}_{\text{eff}}
\approx
\frac{\gamma}{2},
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad gamma-high-snr}
corresponding to the standard asymptotic 3-dB SNR reduction for this equal-hop AF expression.

\subsubsection{Two-hop DF BER}\label{sec:awgn-two-hop-df-ber}

For independent DF hops,
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:df-ber-awgn}
P_e^{\text{DF}}
=
P_{e,1}+P_{e,2}-2P_{e,1}P_{e,2}.
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad df-ber-awgn}

For equal hops,
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:equal-hop-snr}
P_e^{\text{DF}}
=
2P_e(1-P_e),
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad equal-hop-snr}
and for \(P_e\ll1\),
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:df-high-snr-two-hop-penalty}
P_e^{\text{DF}}
\approx
2P_e.
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad df-high-snr-two-hop-penalty}

\subsection{Rayleigh Fading Channel: Theoretical Analysis}\label{sec:rayleigh-fading-channel-theoretical-analysis}

The Rayleigh fading channel is
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:rayleigh-channel}
y=hx+n,
\qquad
h\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,1),
\qquad
n\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma^2).
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad rayleigh-channel}

Since \(h\) is circularly symmetric complex Gaussian,
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:rayleigh-pdf}
f_{|h|}(r)
=
2r e^{-r^2},
\qquad
r\geq0,
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad rayleigh-pdf}
with \(\mathbb{E}[|h|^2]=1\). The instantaneous post-equalization SNR is
\(\gamma_h=|h|^2\text{SNR}\).

\subsubsection{Single-hop BER}\label{sec:rayleigh-single-hop-ber}

Averaging \(Q(\sqrt{2\gamma_h})\) over Rayleigh fading gives \cite[Eq.~14-4-15]{ProakisSalehi2008DigitalComm}
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:rayleigh-ber}
P_e^{\text{Rayleigh}}
=
\frac{1}{2}
\left(
1-\sqrt{\frac{\bar{\gamma}}{1+\bar{\gamma}}}
\right),
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad rayleigh-ber}
where \(\bar{\gamma}=\text{SNR}\).

At high SNR,
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:rayleigh-ber-approx}
P_e^{\text{Rayleigh}}
\approx
\frac{1}{4\bar{\gamma}}.
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad rayleigh-ber-approx}

The resulting \(1/\text{SNR}\) decay is slower than the exponential AWGN decay because deep fades remain significant even at high average SNR.

\subsubsection{Two-hop DF BER}\label{sec:rayleigh-two-hop-df-ber}

For independent equal Rayleigh-faded hops,
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:df-ber-rayleigh}
P_e^{\text{DF,Rayleigh}}
=
2P_e^{\text{Rayleigh}}
\left(
1-P_e^{\text{Rayleigh}}
\right).
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad df-ber-rayleigh}

\section{Channel Coding: Convolutional Codes and Trellis Decoding}\label{sec:channel-coding-convolutional-codes}

The canonical experiments are uncoded. The supplementary study in Section~\ref{sec:coded-block-df} introduces convolutional coding and block-DF. This section summarizes the coding concepts required for that study.

\subsection{Convolutional Encoding: Rate and Constraint Length}\label{sec:convolutional-encoding-rate-constraint-length}

A convolutional encoder maps \(k\) input bits to \(n\) coded bits per trellis step, with rate \cite{ProakisSalehi2008DigitalComm}
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:conv-code-rate}
R=\frac{k}{n}.
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad conv-code-rate}

For the \(k=1\) codes used here, constraint length \(K\) gives
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:conv-trellis-states}
S=2^{K-1}
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad conv-trellis-states}
trellis states. Chapter~\ref{sec:experiments} evaluates \(K\in\{3,5,7\}\), corresponding to \(4\), \(16\), and \(64\) states. Frames are zero-tail terminated by appending \(K-1\) zeros, giving known initial and final trellis states.

\subsection{Maximum-Likelihood Sequence Decoding: The Viterbi Algorithm}\label{sec:viterbi-ml-decoding}

The Viterbi algorithm performs maximum-likelihood sequence decoding by dynamic programming over the trellis \cite{Forney1972MLSE,ProakisSalehi2008DigitalComm}. At each step, it retains the minimum-cost path entering each state, reducing an exponential path search to computation linear in frame length for fixed state count.

For AWGN with Euclidean branch metrics,
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:viterbi-ml-metric}
\hat{c}
=
\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{c\in\mathcal{C}}
\sum_k
|y_k-x(c_k)|^2,
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad viterbi-ml-metric}
where \(\mathcal{C}\) is the set of trellis-valid sequences.

Viterbi minimizes sequence error rather than bit error. Incorrect paths can therefore produce bursts of residual bit errors before remerging with the correct path, a behavior relevant to the coded experiments of Section~\ref{sec:coded-block-df}.

\subsection{Soft-Decision Decoding: The Forward-Backward (BCJR) Algorithm}\label{sec:soft-decision-bcjr-decoding}

BCJR \cite{BahlCockeJelinekRaviv1974BCJR} computes posterior probabilities using forward and backward trellis recursions:
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:bcjr-posterior}
P(s_{k-1}=s',s_k=s\mid\mathbf{y})
\propto
\alpha_{k-1}(s')
\gamma_k(s',s)
\beta_k(s).
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad bcjr-posterior}

Unlike Viterbi's sequence-level criterion, BCJR provides symbol- or bit-level posterior quantities. Marginalizing the transition probabilities yields posterior means or log-likelihood ratios. Section~\ref{sec:coded-block-df} uses this soft information in the soft block-DF relay.

\subsection{Puncturing, Spectral Efficiency, and Rate Adaptation}\label{sec:puncturing-spectral-efficiency}

For code rate \(R\) and constellation size \(M\), spectral efficiency is \cite{ProakisSalehi2008DigitalComm}
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:spectral-efficiency}
\eta
=
R\log_2M
\qquad
\text{[bits/symbol]}.
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad spectral-efficiency}

Puncturing obtains higher rates from a mother code by periodically omitting selected coded bits and treating the corresponding decoder inputs as erasures. Chapter~\ref{sec:experiments} uses this procedure to derive rates \(2/3\) and \(3/4\) from the rate-\(1/2\) mother code.

Because spectral efficiency does not account for failed frames, link adaptation is evaluated using goodput:
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq:goodput}
G
=
\eta(1-\mathrm{FER})
\qquad
\text{[information bits/symbol]}.
\end{equation}
\addcontentsline{equ}{listequations}{\protect\numberline{\theequation}\quad goodput}

Section~\ref{sec:coded-rate-adaptation} maximizes this quantity over the evaluated modulation-and-coding configurations at each SNR, thereby accounting jointly for transmission rate and frame reliability.
