\chapter{Learned Relaying under Unknown and Mismatched Channels}
\label{sec:unknown-channels}

This chapter contains the principal experimental contribution of the thesis.
It asks when a learned relay adds value after the matched, memoryless control of
Chapter~\ref{sec:experiments} has established that it does not reliably improve
on symbol-wise decode-and-forward (DF).  The answer is governed by two
boundaries.  The \emph{identifiability boundary} concerns whether the information
needed to instantiate a model-based rule is supplied or can be estimated
reliably.  The \emph{arithmetic boundary} concerns whether that rule can be
executed within the available computation and decision-delay budgets.

The results do not support universal learned superiority.  Exact-CSI
maximum-likelihood sequence estimation (MLSE) remains the stronger detector for
the specified linear channel and metric, and blind constant-modulus
equalization tracks the learned relay closely when blocks are long enough for
adaptation.  Learning adds value in narrower regimes: memoryless relays fail
when temporal memory invalidates their function class; estimated-channel MLSE
loses its advantage when the pilot estimate becomes unreliable; and a
family-trained relay provides one-shot inference on previously unseen
realizations from that same family without per-block identification.  At larger
memory or constellation order, its bounded-window arithmetic also grows much
more gently than exact trellis search.

\paragraph{The four-layer argument.}
Table~\ref{tbl:layers} separates these effects.  Layer~1 is the matched
memoryless control.  Layer~2 adds memory while matching the learned and
model-based methods to the same fixed channel through different mechanisms:
offline training for the MLP and explicit taps for MLSE.  Layer~3 reduces the
quality of per-block channel information.  Layer~4 removes per-block
identification and tests unseen realizations from the \emph{trained} parametric
family.  Structurally unseen channel families are not tested.

\section{Layer 1: Matched Memoryless Channel}
\label{sec:layer-one}

Layer~1 is the canonical control reported in
Chapter~\ref{sec:experiments}.  It establishes H1--H4 and shows that subsequent
learned gains must be attributed to relaxed channel assumptions or resource
constraints, not to an intrinsic advantage over a well-posed classical rule.

\section{Layer 2: Channel Memory with Available Channel Information}
\label{sec:unknown-channel-experiment}

Hop~1 is
\[
y[n]=h_0x[n]+h_1x[n-1]+h_2x[n-2]+v[n],
\qquad
h=\frac{[1,0.7,0.5]}{\lVert[1,0.7,0.5]\rVert}.
\]
The BPSK MLP uses an 11-sample window, 13 tanh hidden units and 170
parameters.  It is trained at SNRs $\{5,10,15\}$~dB.  Exact-tap MLSE receives
the taps explicitly; the MLP represents the same fixed realization only through
offline training.  Thus Layer~2 compares mappings matched to the same physical
channel, but not identical forms of channel information.

The MLP is retrained with three seeds.  For learned rows, uncertainty is
computed hierarchically: trials are averaged within each seed and a
Student-$t$ interval is formed from the three seed means using
$t_{2,0.975}=4.303$.  Pooling 30 trial values as independent would understate
training uncertainty.  AF and DF have no training-seed population; their
uncertainty is Monte Carlo uncertainty over independent evaluation draws and
should not be described as training variance.

\paragraph{Analytic memoryless-slicer floor.}
Conditioned on $x[n]=+1$, the two previous symbols define four amplitudes
$A(s_1,s_2)=h_0+s_1h_1+s_2h_2$.  Therefore
\begin{equation}
P_e(\gamma)=\frac14\sum_{s_1,s_2\in\{\pm1\}}
\Phi\!\left(\frac{-A(s_1,s_2)}{\sigma}\right),
\qquad
\sigma^2=\frac{1}{2\gamma},\quad
\gamma=10^{\mathrm{SNR_{dB}}/10}.
\label{eq:slicer-floor}
\end{equation}
The normalized amplitudes are $1.6678$, $0.9097$, $0.6065$ and $-0.1516$.
Exactly one pattern reverses the noiseless sign, so the asymptote is $1/4$.
For a general three-tap real channel the floor is $k/4$, where $k$ is the
number of negative amplitudes.  Under the conventional sign normalization
$h_0>0$, their average is $h_0$, so $k\leq3$ and the possible floors are
$0$, $0.25$, $0.5$ and $0.75$.  Without that sign convention, $1$ is also
algebraically possible but is removed by an overall polarity correction.

AF requires a separate calculation.  With positive normalization gain $g$,
the destination slices
$A+n_1+n_2/g$.  This preserves the same four amplitudes with increased noise
variance, so its high-SNR floor is also the fraction of sign-reversing
patterns.  Extra noise can occasionally repair the single negative-amplitude
pattern, explaining why AF approaches the floor differently from hard DF.
The measured and closed-form curves agree to three or four decimals.  The
floor belongs to the evaluated memoryless relay rules and tap vector, not to
ISI generally; exact-tap four-state MLSE has no such floor.

\subsection{QPSK Extension and Corrected Comparator}
\label{sec:qpsk-unknown-channel}

Here Hop~1 includes an additional per-symbol Rayleigh magnitude:
\begin{equation}
y[n]=g[n](h*x)[n]+v[n],\qquad
g[n]=|\mathcal{CN}(0,1)|\ \text{i.i.d.}
\label{eq:qpsk-hop1}
\end{equation}
The training distribution includes this fading law; inference receives the
samples but not the realized $g[n]$.  A trellis supplied only with $h$ is
therefore mismatched.  The full genie comparator receives both $h$ and
$g[n]$.  The earlier apparent MLP-over-genie reversal disappears.

\subsection{Composite Fixed Channel}
\label{sec:composite-channel}

The fixed DBPSK composite cascades ISI, PA saturation and unknown block phase.
The MLP is trained on that structure and uses no explicit analytic impairment
model at inference.  AF and differential detection floor; pilot-LS
Viterbi plus differential decoding gives .0674 against MLP-169's .1073 at
8~dB, and both reach .0025 at 20~dB.  MLP-1153 improves on MLP-169 by .0059
at 10~dB, marginally beyond the combined .0054 interval, but not at 8 or
20~dB.  This is a qualification to H3, not evidence of general capacity gain.

\section{Layer 3: Finite Pilot Information}
\label{sec:partial-posterior}

``Partial posterior'' is used only as regime-map shorthand for incomplete
channel information; the LS procedure does not compute a Bayesian posterior.
At 10~dB, estimated-channel MLSE gives .0285 with 800 pilots, .0335 with 20,
.0545 with 10 and .1235 with 5, against the pilot-free MLP's .0486.  Thus the
measured crossover lies between 20 and 10 pilots.  The five-pilot design is not
asserted to be formally overdetermined because usable LS equations depend on
transient handling; the evidence supports only that its estimates are too noisy
for reliable trellis construction.  The crossover may move with SNR, estimator,
channel statistics and block length.

With 10 pilots, MLP and estimated-channel MLSE have payload BER near .049.
Pilot fraction rises from 1\% at 1000 symbols to 25\% at 40 symbols; this is
payload occupancy, not automatically net throughput.  CMA gives .1723 and
.1653 at lengths 40 and 1000, versus .0645 on a 20,000-symbol adaptation
block.

\section{Layer 4: Unknown Realization Without Per-Block Identification}
\label{sec:blind-channel}

Each test block draws an unseen ISI/PA/phase realization from the same family
used in training.  CMA is likewise pilot-free but adapts online.  The reported
CMA uses a step normalized by input-segment energy, analogous to normalized
LMS stabilization; the divergent fixed-step implementation is excluded as an
implementation defect.  At 20~dB, MLP and CMA give .00262 and .00329, a factor
of 1.26.  They therefore track closely but are not declared equal.
Decision-directed blind MLSE falls to .0374 at 16~dB and rises to .0498 at
20~dB; at 8~dB its CI is $\pm.0201$, against $\pm.0026$ for the MLP and
$\pm.0078$ for CMA.

\label{sec:mmse-baseline}

\section{Arithmetic and Decision Delay}
\label{sec:complexity-viterbi-mlp}

For an $L$-tap channel and $M$-point alphabet, exact MLSE has $M^{L-1}$
states and $M^L$ branches per symbol.  The deployed BPSK MLP is approximately
330 FLOPs per symbol; this must not be equated directly with MAC counts.
Its cost is fixed only after its window, hidden width and output head are
fixed.  Increasing those dimensions raises cost, approximately linearly for
this one-hidden-layer architecture, whereas exact trellis search grows
exponentially in $L$ for fixed $M$.

The reported 30--90$\times$ wall-clock advantage compares vectorized NumPy
matrix multiplication with an interpreted Python trellis loop.  It is an
implementation result, not an algorithmic speed ratio.

No BER improvement is resolved between the tested traceback depths
$D=2,3,15$; survivor merging was not directly measured and is not inferred.
MLSE is therefore both more accurate and no slower than the windowed relay on
this short-memory case.

Under the stated convention, branch metrics cost two real MACs, giving
$2M^L$ MACs per symbol.  The evaluated $W$-sample, $H$-unit QPSK MLP costs
$2WH+4H$.  Equating them gives
\begin{equation}
L^\star=\log_M\!\left(\frac{2WH+4H}{2}\right).
\label{eq:mac-crossover}
\end{equation}
For $M=4,H=8$, this is 3.35 taps at $W=11$, 2.90 at $W=5$ and 3.76 at
$W=21$.  Since $L$ is discrete, the $W=11$ arithmetic crossover lies between
three and four taps.

\paragraph{Synthesis.}
The two-boundary regime map resolves the apparent competition.  When the model
is matched, identifiable and affordable, classical processing is preferred.
Channel memory defeats memoryless relay rules but not correctly informed
sequence detection.  Limited or absent per-block identification creates an
identifiability advantage for amortized within-family learned inference.
Increasing memory or modulation order can separately create an arithmetic
advantage because exact trellis cost grows as $M^L$, while the evaluated
windowed MLP grows much more gently as its stated dimensions are enlarged.
Neither boundary implies universal superiority over correctly informed
classical receivers.
